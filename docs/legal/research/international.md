# International Layer — Research Findings (ToS + Privacy Policy)

Context: California C-corp, C2C secondhand luxury/streetwear marketplace. Sellers primarily US; buyers worldwide; **no establishment outside the US**; Stripe payments; seller-shipped (later EasyPost platform labels); PostHog analytics; ML recommendations + automated counterfeit screening (human review of adverse outcomes) possibly on a **Hetzner VPS in Germany**. Researched 2026-08-25. Sources fetched are listed per topic; ⚠ flags = could not fully confirm on primary sources as of Aug 2026.

---

## 1. GDPR applicability — Art. 3(2) targeting/monitoring test

- **Trigger: NOT immediate.** GDPR applies to a US company with no EU establishment only via Art. 3(2): (a) **offering goods/services to data subjects in the EU** — requires *apparent intention* to target EU users; or (b) **monitoring their behaviour** in the EU. Mere accessibility of the site from the EU, a contact email, or use of English is **insufficient** (EDPB Guidelines 3/2018; Recital 23).
- Targeting indicators (EDPB, cumulative weighing): EU Member State **language or currency** (e.g., EUR prices), **explicit mention of shipping/delivery to EU Member States**, marketing/paid search directed at EU audiences, EU top-level domains (.de/.eu), referencing EU customers, "international" positioning. For this marketplace: **letting sellers ship to EU addresses + accepting EU buyers at checkout is a strong targeting indicator**; add EUR display or EU-directed ads and the test is clearly met.
- Art. 3(2)(b) monitoring = online tracking + profiling/behavioural analysis: **behavioural advertising, cookie/fingerprint tracking, geolocation, personalised recommendations/analytics**. PostHog analytics + ML recommendations applied to EU visitors likely satisfy the monitoring limb **even without "offering"** — this limb is the harder one to design around.
- Practical posture options for the drafts: (i) geo-limit and disclaim EU targeting (then GDPR arguably not triggered — but must actually avoid EU shipping options, EUR, EU marketing, and suppress tracking for EU visitors), or (ii) accept EU buyers and comply. If EU buyers are accepted, assume both limbs are met.
- **Drives:** Privacy Policy "International users / EU-EEA-UK Notice" section (conditional applicability statement); ToS clause on territorial availability of the service; internal decision whether to gate EU signups.
- **Source:** https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/consultation/edpb_guidelines_3_2018_territorial_scope_en.pdf

## 2. GDPR privacy-policy content when it applies (Arts. 13/14, 6, 15–22, 22)

- **Trigger: only once Art. 3(2) is met** (see §1); then disclosures are due **at collection** (Art. 13) / within one month for indirectly obtained data, e.g., data about a buyer supplied by a seller for shipping (Art. 14).
- Art. 13/14 required content: controller identity + contact; **EU representative** (if appointed, §4) and DPO where applicable; **purposes AND the Art. 6 legal basis for each purpose**; legitimate interests pursued; recipients/categories (Stripe, EasyPost, PostHog, Supabase/hosting, Hetzner); **international-transfer destinations and safeguards**; retention periods or criteria; the catalogue of rights; right to withdraw consent; right to complain to a supervisory authority; whether provision is contractual/statutory + consequences of refusal; **existence of automated decision-making incl. profiling with meaningful information about the logic, significance and envisaged consequences**. Format: concise, transparent, plain language, free.
- Art. 6 mapping for marketplace purposes (ICO seven-basis framing; EU GDPR has six): **contract (6(1)(b))** — account, listings, transactions, payments, shipping, messaging; **legitimate interests (6(1)(f))** — fraud/counterfeit prevention, platform security, service improvement, first-party analytics, recommendation personalisation (three-part test: purpose/necessity/balancing — document an LIA); **consent (6(1)(a))** — marketing emails, non-essential cookies/trackers (PostHog for EU visitors); **legal obligation (6(1)(c))** — tax, sanctions screening, law-enforcement requests. Privacy notice **must state the basis per purpose**.
- Data-subject rights to list (Arts. 15–22): access, rectification, erasure, restriction, **portability** (for contract/consent-based data), **objection** (absolute for direct marketing; case-by-case for legitimate-interests processing incl. profiling), plus Art. 22 rights. Include how to exercise (in-app + email) and 1-month response window.
- **Art. 22 analysis for the two ML systems:** (i) *Recommendations* — profiling, but no "legal or similarly significant effects" → **outside Art. 22**; still disclose as profiling/personalisation and honour Art. 21 objection. (ii) *Automated counterfeit screening* — listing removal/account suspension can "similarly significantly affect" a seller (income); **because adverse outcomes get meaningful human review, decisions are not "based solely on automated processing" and Art. 22 is not engaged** — but the human review must be *meaningful* (reviewer has authority and competence to change the decision; not a rubber stamp — ICO/WP251 line). Describe it exactly that way: "automated flagging; no listing is removed or account suspended without human review; you may contest the decision, provide your point of view, and request human re-review."
- If ever a decision were made *solely* automatically with significant effects, it would need an Art. 22(2) gate — most plausibly **necessary for the contract** (fraud/counterfeit prevention as part of marketplace integrity) — plus safeguards (human intervention, right to contest). Prefer keeping the human in the loop instead.
- **Drives:** the entire EU/EEA addendum of the Privacy Policy (purpose/basis table, rights section, ADM section); ToS clauses on listing removal/suspension and appeal of counterfeit determinations.
- **Source:** https://gdpr.eu/privacy-notice/ · https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/automated-decision-making-and-profiling/what-is-automated-individual-decision-making-and-profiling/ · https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/a-guide-to-lawful-basis/

## 3. International transfers (GDPR Ch. V) — SCCs and the EU–US DPF (status Aug 2026)

- **DPF current status: STANDS.** Adequacy decision of 10 July 2023 remains in force. The EU General Court **dismissed Latombe v Commission (T-553/23) on 3 Sept 2025**, upholding the DPF (DPRC independence and bulk-collection safeguards sufficient). **Latombe appealed to the CJEU on 31 Oct 2025 (Case C-703/25 P); appeal pending, no hearing date** — DPF valid meanwhile but not a permanent foundation. ⚠ Secondary reporting of PCLOB quorum loss and FISA §702 reauthorization wobbles as pressure points — not verified on primary sources; treat as risk colour, not fact.
- Realistic options for a small US company: (i) **self-certify to the DPF** (requires FTC jurisdiction — fine for a C-corp marketplace; annual fee + Privacy Shield-style commitments), or (ii) **2021 modernised SCCs** (four modules) with a **transfer impact assessment + supplementary measures** (Schrems II). Belt-and-braces: many US companies certify to DPF **and** keep SCCs as fallback given C-703/25 P. SCCs may not be modified in substance.
- **What is a "transfer" here (EDPB Guidelines 05/2021, three cumulative criteria):** direct collection from EU users by the US company is **NOT a Ch. V transfer** (Art. 3(2) applies to the processing, but no exporter). A transfer needs disclosure by a GDPR-subject exporter to a separate controller/processor in a third country — **regardless of whether the importer is already subject to GDPR**.
- **Germany VPS implications:** data flowing **from the Hetzner-hosted ML backend (EU processor) back to the US company = an EU→US transfer** requiring safeguards (SCC Module 4 processor→controller, or DPF); onward flows US→EU to Hetzner make Hetzner a **processor needing an Art. 28 DPA**. The Commission was still developing a dedicated SCC set for importers already subject to GDPR — until adopted, use existing SCCs/DPF. (US→EU direction is not restricted by GDPR itself; no US federal export restriction on ordinary personal data to Germany.)
- Privacy policy must name the transfer mechanism(s) relied on and how to get a copy (Arts. 13(1)(f)).
- **Drives:** Privacy Policy "International Data Transfers" section; DPA with Hetzner + SCC/DPF decision; vendor list (Stripe, PostHog — check their DPF certifications).
- **Source:** https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/international-dimension-data-protection/eu-us-data-transfers_en · https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/international-dimension-data-protection/standard-contractual-clauses-scc_en · https://iapp.org/news/a/european-general-court-dismisses-latombe-challenge-upholds-eu-us-data-privacy-framework · https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/35459-latombe-filed-appeal-against-general-court-dismissal-of-challenge-to-european-unionunited-states-data-protection-framework-adequacy-decision-in-latombe-v-commission · https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/documents/2023-02/edpb_guidelines_05-2021_interplay_between_the_application_of_art3-chapter_v_of_the_gdpr_v2_en_0.pdf

## 4. Art. 27 EU representative (+ UK equivalent)

- **Trigger: same moment GDPR applies via Art. 3(2)** — any non-EU controller/processor caught by Art. 3(2) must designate in writing a representative established in an EU Member State where relevant data subjects are, **unless** the Art. 27(2)(a) exemption applies: processing that is **occasional**, does **not** include large-scale special-category/criminal data, **and** is unlikely to risk rights and freedoms — all three cumulatively.
- EDPB reads "occasional" narrowly; an online marketplace **systematically and continuously** processing EU buyers' data (accounts, orders, tracking) is **not occasional** — the exemption is realistically unavailable once EU buyers are a regular part of the business, even at small volume. ⚠ Grey zone if EU purchases are rare one-offs and no monitoring runs on EU visitors; document the assessment either way.
- Representative duties: named **in the privacy notice** with contact details; acts as contact point for supervisory authorities and data subjects; holds Art. 30 records. Commercial rep services run ~€500–1,500/yr. Non-appointment is finable (Art. 83(4): up to €10M/2%).
- **UK equivalent:** UK GDPR Art. 27 imposes a mirrored UK-representative duty on non-UK controllers caught by UK GDPR Art. 3(2). ⚠ **The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 does not appear to have removed the UK representative requirement** (the abolition was in the lapsed 2024 DPDI Bill; no fetched DUAA summary lists Art. 27 among the changes, and UK-rep services still operate in 2026) — **verify against the DUAA text/ICO guidance before drafting**.
- **Drives:** Privacy Policy identity block ("Our EU representative is… / Our UK representative is…"); go/no-go decision on serving EU users before appointing.
- **Source:** https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/consultation/edpb_guidelines_3_2018_territorial_scope_en.pdf · https://eushield.eu/gdpr-article-27-guide/ (secondary) · https://cognisys.co.uk/blog/data-use-and-access-act-changes/ (DUAA change list)

## 5. UK GDPR + PECR mirror (ICO)

- **Trigger:** UK GDPR applies via its own Art. 3(2) targeting/monitoring test for users in the UK (same analysis as §1, substituting UK indicators — GBP, UK shipping, UK-directed marketing).
- **PECR cookies:** consent (UK GDPR standard — unambiguous positive action; no cookie walls-by-inertia, no implied consent by browsing) required before setting non-essential cookies; **"strictly necessary" exemption** covers only service-essential cookies (cart, auth/security, load balancing) — **not analytics** under classic PECR.
- **DUAA 2025 changes (main provisions commenced 5 Feb 2026):** new consent exceptions for **first-party statistics/analytics**, appearance/functionality preferences, and certain strictly-necessary purposes — conditioned on clear information and an **opt-out**; advertising/cross-site tracking still needs consent. **PECR fines raised to UK GDPR levels (£17.5M/4%)**. Also: Art. 22 ADM restrictions narrowed to mainly special-category data (new safeguards regime — inform, explain, human review, contest); "recognised legitimate interests" basis; "data protection test" for transfers. PostHog configured first-party/analytics-only may qualify for the new UK statistics exception (unlike EU ePrivacy) — but keep a consent banner for EU users regardless.
- ⚠ PECR's application to a US site serving UK users is asserted by ICO practice (storing/accessing info on UK terminal equipment) but the fetched page did not address territorial scope explicitly.
- **Drives:** Privacy Policy UK addendum + Cookie Policy (separate EU vs UK consent logic); ToS governing-law carve-out for UK consumers.
- **Source:** https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guide-to-pecr/cookies-and-similar-technologies/ · https://www.cliffordchance.com/insights/resources/blogs/talking-tech/en/articles/2025/09/uk-ico-s-updated-guidance-for-new-exceptions-to-cookie-consents-.html · https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2025/7/the-uk-data-use-and-access-act-2025 · https://cognisys.co.uk/blog/data-use-and-access-act-changes/

## 6. EU Digital Services Act (DSA) — what actually applies to a tiny US marketplace

- **Trigger:** DSA applies to non-EU providers only with a **"substantial connection to the Union"** (Art. 3(e)): EU establishment, a **significant number of recipients** in a Member State relative to population, **or targeting activities** at Member States (language/currency, local ads, app-store availability). Mere accessibility ≠ in scope — same practical lever as GDPR §1.
- **Micro/small-enterprise exemptions are broad:** Art. 19(1) disapplies Section 3 (online-platform duties: internal complaints, out-of-court dispute bodies, trusted flaggers, transparency reports, ad transparency) and **Art. 29(1) disapplies Section 4 — the marketplace trader-traceability regime (Arts. 30–32) — for providers qualifying as micro or small enterprises** (Rec. 2003/361: micro <10 staff & ≤€2M turnover; small <50 & ≤€10M), unless a VLOP. **A tiny US marketplace is exempt from Arts. 30–32 while it stays under those thresholds** (grace period after outgrowing them).
- **What is NOT exempt, if in scope:** (i) **Art. 13 legal representative in the EU** — required of every third-country provider offering services in the Union, **no size exemption** (a real cost trigger — factor into the EU go/no-go alongside GDPR Art. 27; the two roles can often be bundled with one provider); (ii) **Art. 16 notice-and-action** (all hosting providers): easy, electronic, clearly identifiable reporting; substantiated notices (reasoned explanation, exact URL, notifier name/email except CSAM, good-faith statement); confirm receipt; decide timely, diligently, non-arbitrarily; communicate decision + redress; disclose whether automated means were used; (iii) **Art. 17 statement of reasons** for removals/suspensions incl. whether automated (dovetails with the counterfeit-screening appeal flow in §2); (iv) Art. 14 T&C transparency incl. content-moderation policies, and Art. 11/12 contact points.
- **Arts. 30–32 for context (post-exemption future):** collect + verify trader name, address, ID, payment details, self-certification before allowing trader listings (KYBC — applies to **traders** only, not consumer sellers); design interface for trader disclosures (31); notify consumers who bought an illegal product from a trader (32).
- **Drives:** ToS content-moderation/reporting clause (Art. 16-compatible notice channel + appeal), ToS suspension/removal clause (statement of reasons), ToS "EU users" clause naming the DSA legal representative if in scope; roadmap note for trader onboarding at scale.
- **Source:** https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065

## 7. EU consumer law for C2C platforms (CRD, Omnibus, P2B)

- **14-day withdrawal right applies only to TRADER→consumer distance contracts** (CRD 2011/83/EU Arts. 2, 9): a private individual selling their own used clothes is not a "trader," so **EU buyers get no statutory withdrawal right in C2C sales** — the platform can honestly say so, and must (next bullet). If the platform itself ever sells (e.g., authenticated-resale inventory) or professional sellers join, CRD applies to those sales (with Art. 16 exceptions: personalised goods; unsealed hygiene-sealed goods).
- **Omnibus Directive 2019/2161 → CRD Art. 6a — trigger: offering an online marketplace to EU consumers.** Before an EU consumer is bound, the marketplace must disclose: **whether the seller is a trader or non-trader, based on the seller's own declaration**; that **EU consumer-protection rights do not apply to the contract when the seller is not a trader**; how contract obligations split between seller and platform; and main ranking parameters (also UCPD-driven). Build: a "private seller / professional seller" self-declaration at listing + a badge and notice at checkout.
- **P2B Regulation 2019/1150 — trigger: BUSINESS users established in the EU offering to EU consumers via the platform, regardless of where the platform is established.** Pure C2C = out of scope; **one EU-based professional seller pulls it in.** Duties: plain-language T&Cs with grounds for suspension/termination; **15-day advance notice of T&C changes**; statement of reasons for restriction/suspension (30 days' notice for termination); **Art. 5 ranking transparency — describe main ranking parameters AND any possibility to influence ranking against direct/indirect remuneration (the paid "boost" placement) in the T&Cs**; small enterprises are exempt from the internal complaint-handling system and mediator designation (Arts. 11–12) but **not** from Arts. 3–5.
- **Drives:** ToS seller-classification clause + trader self-declaration; checkout disclosure banner ("private seller — EU consumer rights don't apply"); ToS "Boosted listings & ranking" clause (also mirrors DSA Art. 26(3)/CRD 6a ranking disclosure); ToS suspension-notice mechanics for EU business sellers.
- **Source:** https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:02011L0083-20220528 · https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32019R1150

## 8. EU General Product Safety Regulation 2023/988 (applies from 13 Dec 2024)

- **Trigger:** the GPSR marketplace regime (Art. 22) binds "providers of online marketplaces" — defined around allowing consumers to conclude distance contracts **with traders** — with **no micro/small exemption**, when serving the EU market. **Purely C2C supply is outside GPSR**: products supplied by private individuals outside a commercial activity are not "placed on the market." ⚠ Inference flagged: a platform hosting only non-trader sellers arguably isn't a GPSR "online marketplace" at all; the duty crystallises once trader-sellers are allowed for EU buyers.
- **Second-hand goods ARE covered** when supplied commercially, **except** products the consumer cannot reasonably expect to meet modern safety standards: **antiques**, and goods **explicitly presented as "to be repaired or reconditioned prior to use."** Ordinary used clothing sold by traders = in scope.
- Art. 22 duties (if/when triggered): register in the **EU Safety Gate Portal** and name a single contact point; internal product-safety processes; **expeditious removal** of dangerous-product listings on actual knowledge and processing of Safety Gate notices (2 working days); cooperate with market-surveillance authorities; random checks against Safety Gate; ensure traders provide traceability info before listing goes live.
- Art. 19: traders selling online to EU consumers must display in the offer: manufacturer name + contact, product identifier, warnings/safety info — a listing-field design constraint for trader accounts.
- **Drives:** ToS prohibited/recalled-items clause + safety notice-and-takedown; seller rules for trader accounts (listing data fields); "products sold as-is for repair" labelling option; roadmap item (Safety Gate registration) gated on admitting EU-facing trader sellers.
- **Source:** https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32023R0988

## 9. Cross-border shipping & customs for C2C sales

- **Default allocation for ToS: buyer = importer of record.** C2C parcels move DAP/DDU (buyer handles import clearance and pays duties/taxes/broker fees) unless the platform or seller opts into DDP (seller/platform bears them). ⚠ trade.gov Incoterms page fetched but thin — allocation point is standard ICC Incoterms 2020 practice; state it contractually rather than by citation.
- **EU inbound (buyer side):** import **VAT is due on ALL goods** entering the EU (the €22 exemption is gone). **IOSS** lets a seller/marketplace charge VAT at checkout for consignments **≤ €150**; a **marketplace that "facilitates" distance sales of imported goods ≤ €150 becomes the deemed supplier liable for the VAT**, and a non-EU business needs an EU-established intermediary to register. Without IOSS, the carrier/post collects VAT from the buyer at import plus a clearance fee.
- **€150 customs-duty threshold: GONE as of 1 July 2026.** Council gave final approval 11 Feb 2026: duty-free relief for <€150 parcels abolished; interim regime (1 Jul 2026 → ~2028) levies a **flat €3 customs duty per tariff-category of item in the parcel** (silk blouse + wool blouse = €6), full tariff treatment once the EU Customs Data Hub arrives (~2028). Draft ToS/pricing copy must not promise "no customs duty under €150."
- **US inbound (context for foreign sellers later / returns from abroad):** the **$800 de minimis exemption is suspended for ALL countries** — EO 14324 (eff. 29 Aug 2025), made indefinite for non-postal modes by CBP interim final rule of 24 Jun 2026, and **permanently terminated by statute effective 1 July 2027** (One Big Beautiful Bill Act). All low-value imports now require informal/formal entry with duties.
- **Drives:** ToS "International Purchases" clause (buyer is importer of record; duties/taxes/fees are buyer's responsibility; no delivery-time guarantees through customs; failed-customs-refusal handling); Privacy Policy (customs data shared with carriers); future IOSS/deemed-supplier decision before enabling EU checkout at scale.
- **Source:** https://vat-one-stop-shop.ec.europa.eu/index_en · https://taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu/news/e-commerce-150-eur-customs-duty-exemption-threshold-be-removed-2026-2025-11-13_en · https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2025/12/12/customs-council-agrees-to-levy-customs-duty-on-small-parcels-as-of-1-july-2026/ · https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/02/11/council-gives-final-green-light-to-new-customs-duty-rules-for-small-parcels/ · https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/24/2026-12670/indefinite-suspension-of-the-de-minimis-exemption-for-merchandise-arriving-through-all-modes-other · https://www.trade.gov/know-your-incoterms

## 10. US export controls & sanctions (immediate — applies from day one, no EU nexus needed)

- **Comprehensively sanctioned as of Aug 2026: Cuba, Iran, North Korea, and the Crimea/Donetsk/Luhansk regions of Ukraine** (OFAC programs + EAR 746.2/.4/.6/.7). **Syria is NO LONGER comprehensively sanctioned**: EO 14312 (30 Jun 2025) revoked the program; the Syrian Sanctions Regulations were removed 25 Aug 2025; targeted **PAARSS** (31 CFR 569) list-based sanctions remain (Assad network, traffickers, ISIS/AQ, Iran proxies). Russia/Belarus and Venezuela: extensive but **targeted/sectoral**, not comprehensive. Keep the ToS list maintainable ("jurisdictions embargoed by the U.S., currently including…").
- **SDN screening expectation is real for e-commerce:** the OFAC Amazon settlement (Jul 2020, $134,523) faulted screening that missed spelling variants ("Yalta, Krimea"), sanctioned-jurisdiction addresses, and SDN name matches; OFAC expects **automated screening of parties/addresses, IP-geolocation controls, and routine testing** "commensurate with speed and scale." Strict liability applies. For B0-scale: block checkout/shipping to embargoed jurisdictions, screen names/addresses against the SDN list (Stripe handles card-side screening but the platform still owns shipping-side risk), log decisions.
- **Luxury-goods export bans (confirmed):** EAR **§ 746.8(a)(7)** — license required (policy of denial) to export/reexport any "luxury good" in **Supplement No. 5 to Part 746** to or within **Russia or Belarus**, or to designated oligarchs worldwide — Supp. 5 covers apparel, footwear, leather goods, jewellery, watches: i.e., **this marketplace's core inventory cannot be shipped to Russia/Belarus**. ⚠ Per-item dollar thresholds in Supp. 5 not verified (746.10 was moved/reserved in the 2025 EAR restructuring — cite 746.8(a)(7), not 746.10). **North Korea:** § 746.4 — license required, **general policy of denial for luxury goods**.
- Standard ToS clause this drives: user representations (not an SDN/blocked person; not located in embargoed jurisdictions; won't export items in violation of U.S. export controls/EAR incl. luxury-goods rules), plus platform right to refuse/cancel/freeze transactions for sanctions compliance.
- **Drives:** ToS "Sanctions & Export Compliance" clause + eligibility reps; ToS shipping-destination restrictions (no Russia/Belarus/embargoed territories); ops requirement for SDN/geo screening described in Privacy Policy (legal-obligation basis).

## 11. CITES / U.S. Fish & Wildlife — exotic-skin fashion

- **Trigger: any international shipment (import or export) of wildlife products — no volume threshold.** Python, crocodile, alligator, many lizards, some furs are CITES-listed (App. I or II); international movement requires **CITES permits** (export permit from origin country; App. I effectively barred from commercial trade), obtained via FWS ePermits.
- On top of CITES: **every wildlife import/export must be declared to FWS (Form 3-177 via eDecs)**; **commercial shipments require an FWS import/export licence** (~$100/yr) and must clear through **designated ports** (or a Designated Port Exception Permit); inspection fees apply; undeclared shipments risk seizure and penalties (Lacey Act/50 CFR). Limited personal-effects exemptions exist for accompanying baggage but generally **not** for mailed commercial sales between individuals.
- Practical upshot — why platforms (Grailed-style) **ban international shipment of exotic-skin items**: an individual C2C seller cannot realistically obtain CITES export permits, an FWS licence, and designated-port clearance for one parcel; the platform cannot verify species/permits at listing time; seizure risk lands on buyer and seller. **Domestic US-to-US sale of lawfully acquired exotic leather remains generally permissible** (state-law caveats, e.g., California python).
- **Drives:** ToS Prohibited & Restricted Items clause: "items made of CITES-listed species (python, crocodile, alligator, certain furs) may be listed for **domestic shipment only**; international shipment prohibited"; counterfeit/materials screening taxonomy; seller shipping rules.
- **Source:** https://www.fws.gov/program/office-of-law-enforcement/information-importers-exporters

## 12. Canada (PIPEDA + Quebec Law 25) and Australia — short section

- **PIPEDA — trigger:** collection/use/disclosure of personal information **in the course of commercial activity**; Canadian courts/OPC apply it to foreign companies with a **"real and substantial connection" to Canada** (⚠ the connection test is settled case law — A.T. v. Globe24h, Lawson — but was not stated on the fetched OPC page). Obligations: the **10 fair-information principles** (consent, openness/plain-language policy, access/correction, safeguards, accountability); **no localization — accountability model for cross-border transfers** (comparable protection via contract, and say in the policy that data is processed in the US); breach reporting to OPC + individuals for "real risk of significant harm" (not on fetched page; per Act s.10.1).
- **Quebec Law 25 — trigger: doing business in / processing personal info of people in Quebec, no size threshold.** Needs: a designated **person in charge of personal information** (default: CEO; title + contact published on the site); published plain-language privacy policy (in practice, **French** for Quebec consumers — Bill 96 adjacency); express consent for sensitive data + consent for non-essential cookies; **assessment before communicating personal info outside Quebec** (adequate-protection analysis — covers the US hosting); confidentiality-incident register + notify CAI/individuals on risk of serious injury; transparency + deactivation option for tech that identifies/locates/profiles (the recommendation engine); penalties up to **CA$25M or 4% of worldwide turnover** + private right of action.
- **Australia — trigger: "carrying on business in Australia"** (Privacy Act; the 2022 amendment removed the old requirement to also collect/hold data *in* Australia). Mere accessibility isn't enough; indicators mirror GDPR targeting (offering shipping to AU, AU in country dropdowns, cookies on AU devices). ⚠ The **AU$3M small-business turnover exemption** should shield a tiny marketplace initially — exemption exists in the Act but was not confirmed on the fetched page (and is slated for removal in ongoing reforms); penalties now up to AU$50M. One line in the policy ("users in Australia") is enough at this stage.
- **Drives:** Privacy Policy "Users in Canada" subsection (US-processing disclosure, PIPEDA rights, Quebec officer + French availability note) and one-line Australia acknowledgement; cookie-consent scope decision (Quebec opt-in).
